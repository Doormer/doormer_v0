How to learn from a worked solution (not just copy it)

A worked solution is most useful when you treat it like a coach: pause, predict, explain, and then try without it.

Updated 10 October 2026 · 5 min read

The copying trap

A worked solution can be a brilliant teacher. It can also trick you. When every line is already written, the answer seems to flow. You nod along, copy the algebra, and think, "Yes, that makes sense." Then the next question changes one number or asks for a different part, and suddenly the method disappears.

Psychologists call this an illusion of competence: the feeling of understanding that comes from recognising someone else's work. Recognition is not the same as being able to do it. The fix is not to avoid worked solutions. The fix is to use them actively, so your brain has to make decisions instead of just watching.

Assessed work has different rules

Follow your school's instructions for assessed work. Homework practice is for learning the method, but work handed in for assessment must follow the rules your teacher gives you.

Try first, even briefly

Before opening a solution, spend a few minutes on the question. You do not have to finish it. Write what you know and what is being asked. Circle the useful numbers. Sketch the shape again. For a science question, note the quantity you need and the units it should have. For a maths question, write the formula or method you think might apply.

This short attempt gives the worked solution something to connect to. If you got stuck because you did not know which formula to use, the first line of the solution will mean more. If you made a sign error, you will notice exactly where your path split from the worked one.

Use the plan before the steps

Do not rush straight to the final answer. Start with the plan. A good plan tells you the shape of the solution before the details arrive. For example: "Find the missing angle, use trigonometry to find the height, then use area." That is more useful than a bare string of calculations, because it shows why each step exists.

After reading the plan, close your eyes or cover the screen and say the plan back in your own words. If you cannot say it simply, read it again. The aim is not to memorise the exact sentence. The aim is to know the route before you walk it.

Predict and explain each step

Before you reveal the next step, predict what it will do. You might ask, "Will it substitute into a formula?" or "Will it rearrange for x?" Your prediction can be wrong. That is still useful, because the difference between your guess and the solution shows what you need to learn.

After each step, explain it in your own words. Michelene Chi and colleagues found in 1989 that students who explained worked examples to themselves learned more from them. A useful explanation is small and plain: "We divide by 3 because the coefficient of x is 3" or "This angle matches because alternate angles are equal."

Use any "Why this works" note as a prompt, not as a script. Read it, then turn away and say it differently. If you can explain why the step is allowed, you are much closer to being able to use the method in a new question.

Redo and stretch the question

Worked examples help most when a topic is new. John Sweller described this as the worked-example effect: seeing a clear example can reduce the load while you are learning the basics. But once the method starts to make sense, switch towards solving on your own. That is where the learning gets stronger.

Cover the solution and redo the question from a blank start. This is retrieval practice: pulling the method from memory instead of rereading it. Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke found in 2006 that practising recall beat rereading for remembering later. If you get stuck, peek at one step, cover it again, and continue.

Next, change the numbers and try a similar question. If the original used a 12 metre side, try 15 metres. If it asked for area, ask what would change if it asked for a missing side. Finally, check another way: estimate the size of the answer, put it back into the question, or compare it with class notes.

Come back after a few days. Spaced practice works better than doing the same type of question ten times in one sitting and never seeing it again. Save useful questions so you can revisit them before a test.

A 10-minute routine

  1. Spend two minutes trying the question and writing what is asked.
  2. Read the plan, then say the plan back without looking.
  3. Reveal one step and predict the next move before continuing.
  4. Explain each step in your own words, including why it is allowed.
  5. Cover the solution and redo the question from scratch.
  6. Change one number or ask a similar question and try again.
  7. Check the answer by estimating, substituting back, or using class notes.
  8. Save the question and revisit it in a few days.
