A parent's guide to AI homework help

AI homework tools can support practice, but the useful habit is still the same: your teen needs to do the thinking and explain the method.

What AI homework helpers do

AI homework help now covers several different tools. A photo solver reads a question from an image and gives a worked solution. A chatbot answers typed questions or explains a topic. A tutor-style app may ask questions back, give hints, or walk through a method. Some tools do all three.

For parents, the important question is not "Is AI allowed in the house?" It is "What kind of thinking is my child doing while using it?" A calculator can support maths or hide weak number sense. A worked example can teach a method or become copying. AI homework help has the same split. The real risk is skipping the thinking.

A useful rule of thumb

If your teen can show the next similar question without the app, the tool probably supported learning. If the answer only exists on the screen, it probably replaced the learning.

What good use looks like

Good use starts before the tool opens. Your teen should try the question first, even if that only means writing what is known, what is asked, and where they are stuck. That small attempt makes the help more targeted and keeps the question from becoming a button press.

Good use also leaves a trail. You should see working, not just an answer. Your teen should be able to explain the key step afterwards: why they rearranged an equation, why a force diagram points that way, or why a unit changed from centimetres to metres. They might use the tool to check their answer, compare a method, or understand a mistake.

A strong sign is transfer. After one supported question, can they do a similar one with different numbers? Can they explain which part changed and which part stayed the same? That matters more than whether tonight's worksheet is finished quickly.

Warning signs

Watch for answers written down without working. Watch for a method your teen cannot explain five minutes later. Watch for homework marks rising while test marks fall, because that can mean support is doing the work at home while independent recall is not growing. Also watch for the app being used for every question, including ones your teen could attempt alone.

None of these signs means you need a dramatic ban. They are reasons to reset the habit. Move the tool later in the routine. Ask for a first attempt on paper. Keep the app for checking, hints, and explanations after real effort.

Questions to ask your teen

The best parent questions are calm and specific. Try "Show me how you'd do the next one." Try "What was the key step?" Try "Where did you get stuck?" Try "Can you do a similar one without it?" These questions are not a courtroom cross-examination. They are a quick check that the method belongs to your teen, not just to the app.

If they cannot answer, go smaller. Ask them to explain only one line. Ask what the units should be. Ask which number in the question was most useful. A teen who feels trapped may shut down; a teen who can explain one step can often rebuild confidence.

School rules and assessment

Homework practice is different from assessed work. Practice is where students learn, get stuck, use examples, and ask for help. Assessment is where the school checks what the student can do under its rules. Keep those categories clear at home.

In New Zealand, work submitted for NCEA internal assessment must be the student's own. In Australia, rules vary by state and school, so check with the teacher. If your teen is not sure whether AI help is allowed for a task, the safest move is to ask before using it.

Checking an app

Before any homework app becomes normal in your family, check the basics. You do not need to be a technology expert. You need clear answers to practical questions.

  • What personal data does the app collect?
  • Is it made for your child's age?
  • Are ads personalised, or based on browsing history?
  • Can you delete the account or data?
  • What does it cost now, and what might it cost later?
  • Are there subscriptions or in-app purchases?
  • Does it show working and checks, or mainly final answers?

A family agreement

A simple agreement beats a long lecture. For example: try for ten minutes first; write where you got stuck; use AI for a hint, explanation or check; explain one step out loud afterwards; never use it for assessed work unless the teacher has said how it may be used.

Make the agreement visible and revisitable. It should not be a punishment. It is a way to keep homework support fair, useful and less stressful. If the agreement is not working, adjust it together after a week.
